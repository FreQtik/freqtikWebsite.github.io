What Impulse Anvil Does

Impulse Anvil is for making impulse responses, not only loading them.

A normal convolution workflow is often:

pick an IR → put sound through it

Anvil adds the creative part before that:

load IRs or recordings → change them → combine them → listen → Bake a new IR

You can start with almost anything useful

Try:

  • a room or cabinet IR;
  • another IR you already designed;
  • a metal hit;
  • a voice or breath;
  • a scrape;
  • a field recording;
  • a short noise or texture;
  • a tiny section taken from a much longer recording.

The source does not need to sound like a reverb before you load it.

What Anvil lets you do

  • A and B hold two independent starting materials.
  • Time makes them shorter or longer.
  • Color adds changed copies back into the IR.
  • Texture repeats that Color idea in deeper layers.
  • Morph decides how A and B become one new response.
  • A→B Lerp lets the IR itself move from A toward B over time.
  • EDIT searches, cuts, reverses and fades the part you actually want.
  • EQ and Width shape the final IR.
  • Bake saves what you made as a reusable WAV.

Why Bake matters

Bake turns an experiment into new raw material.

You can make:

A + B → C

then load C back in:

C + D → E

and continue.

That is why Impulse Anvil can create results that no single original IR contained.

Technical background

Convolution can use any compatible audio waveform as its impulse-response data. Short material often behaves more like a complex tonal/filter fingerprint; longer material can add stronger time, resonance and decay behavior. Anvil uses that freedom deliberately for sound design instead of limiting the source material to measured acoustic spaces.
